Capture · File format

Straight-out-of-camera is the dream; JPEG is the decision

Igor has been testing how autoHDR responds to different inputs, and it behaves completely differently depending on what it is fed. HEIF files — 10-bit, higher dynamic range, lighter than JPEG — looked the best on paper but came out poorly through autoHDR. The likely reason: the AI is trained on old-school 8-bit JPEGs and doesn’t understand the high-dynamic-range HEIF data.

The bigger HEIF problem was lens distortion. The camera auto-corrects barrel distortion on JPEG (a Canon 14–35 was the example), but on HEIF the correction is lost and walls come out curved — unacceptable for real-estate interiors. Canon’s own guidance is to disable perspective/distortion correction only when shooting extreme angles.

The shared ambition is a clean straight-out-of-camera (SOOC) JPEG that can go directly to HDR — fewer steps, less room for error, faster for everyone. Igor will keep testing to get SOOC quality good enough.

Decision · per Christian’s direction

For now we keep it simple: 100% JPEG exported from Lightroom → Quincy Portal. RAW files are retained as a security backup only and won’t normally be used.

Capture · Bracketing

Five brackets in, three brackets out

The pain

autoHDR only really understands three brackets. Feed it five and it often picks the wrong ones — fully dark or blown out — instead of the three usable exposures. Mark then has to hand-pick three of five for every photo, which doesn’t scale on busy days (e.g. 10 shoots to edit).

The direction

Igor & Christian have the exposure discipline (e.g. +2 stops, no clipping) to shoot three manual brackets and skip the cherry-pick. Contractors are different — from them we still want five brackets plus RAW security backups, to be safe.

Open question

How do we get contractors to a usable three-bracket output without policing every shoot? Likely answer: let contractors over-deliver (5 + RAW) and solve the selection downstream, rather than trusting their brackets.

Workflow · On-camera ratings

Decide the selects once, at the shoot

Igor’s idea is to cull on the camera while still on site with the agent. A single mapped button does rate + protect in one press, so as he scrolls the dial with the agent (“you like this one?”) he flags the keeper of each bracket — usually the middle exposure.

A simple rating code carries the meaning forward:

★ One star

Select / keeper

This frame is part of the final delivery. Filtering by one star in Lightroom surfaces exactly the photos that need uploading — no relying on memory across ten later shoots.

★★ Two stars

Has an editing note

The agent asked for something (e.g. “remove this sign”). Two stars marks frames that need an instruction to QA, so they can be batched and annotated quickly.

Decision

Embed the star rating in the Lightroom JPEG export metadata. The portal will extract the rating so the rating done on-site drives selection from the very start. (Igor to confirm the metadata export setting.)

Workflow · Colour & export

Warmer balance, and a one-click export

On import, Igor applies a Lightroom auto white balance on top of the camera’s — the result is noticeably warmer and more pleasing than the camera’s cooler reading, and warmer than autoHDR (which tends to a blue tint). Working the colour at this higher-quality stage beats tweaking a finished JPEG, which degrades it. A side idea for in-camera custom white balance: a light-meter app with a neutral white cube, or a clear Lee filter / Sony grey cap hovered over the lens — roughly 70–80% accurate, enough to get to a ballpark. The caveat is it has to be redone per room, since green from a garden or magenta from brickwork shifts the reading.

The export itself is being streamlined into a single Lightroom preset:

01

RAW → JPEG

Full resolution, no compression.

02

Straight to Dropbox

Export to the server folder, not the laptop.

03

Name the folder

Short address + input / output counts.

04

Show in Finder

Dropbox app uploads while you work elsewhere.

Detail worth keeping

Folder naming carries the counts: e.g. “…55” means 55 input files; an output count (e.g. 11) tells Mark how many finished photos to expect. Most photos are 5 exposures, but a focus stack can be 10 — so both numbers are useful. The portal can read these counts straight off the folder.

Workflow · Notes to QA

Annotate in the portal, not in a pile of screenshots

Today

To tell Mark what to fix, the team screenshots a frame, annotates it, saves it into a folder, and repeats for every photo that needs a note. It’s fiddly and easily forgotten — roughly half an hour a job.

In the portal

Highlight and draw the instruction directly on the frame in Quincy Portal. The markup is an overlay only — it generates a thumbnail and never alters the file Mark receives for editing.

Impact

Estimated ~30 minutes saved per job, plus a “ready for raw review” notification to Mark when the photographer is done.

Platform · Quincy Portal

One portal, four roles, on Cloudflare

The prototype (quincy.flamingfire.my) is non-functional but lets us visualise the system and make cheap changes now, while the foundation is still soft. It runs on Cloudflare Pages with R2 storage — the decisive advantage is free egress / bandwidth, which matters enormously for high-res photos (50–100 MB each) and video. Likely the free tier, or roughly $20/month — a fraction of what AWS would charge, and a path to cutting Dropbox, Pixieset and Vimeo costs. Cloudflare can do this because it grew from a DNS provider into a global CDN, so the distribution network is already built.

Platform · Integrations

Dropbox is the spine — for now

Everything currently links through Dropbox, and that’s deliberate. Tonomo creates a job under the agent’s name and — when a photographer is assigned — auto-creates a Dropbox upload folder for that job. The plan is for Igor to export from Lightroom straight into that waiting Dropbox folder, because the Dropbox desktop app guarantees the upload completes in the background while he works on something else.

Decision · upload reliability

Avoid browser uploads. A browser tab must stay open and can silently fail or stop halfway — a real risk when Igor uploads between jobs on a 5G hotspot rather than at home. The Dropbox app gives the guarantee that a file is 100% uploaded.

autoHDR connects via Dropbox too: it can watch a folder, process new RAW/JPEG files automatically, and push the edited results back to an “output” folder beside the inputs. Someone still needs to configure that watch (Terry to look into it). A direct Quincy Portal ↔ autoHDR API integration is possible but is a future project — roughly 1–2 weeks to build plus debugging — so it’s not happening soon.
